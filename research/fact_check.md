# Fact check: "Stranded in the middle of the ocean with nothing around you"

Scenario: one person in warm-ish open ocean water, no boat, no life jacket, drifting for about 2 days, then rescued.
Researched November 2026. Verdicts: **Accurate** / **Needs rewording** / **Wrong**.

Access notes:
- rnli.org returned HTTP 403 to every fetch. The RNLI wording below comes from RNLI press releases republished word for word, the RNLI Jersey station site, and the joint Met Office/RNLI/RLSS page. Check https://rnli.org/float once before you lock the script.
- NCBI Bookshelf (StatPearls) and PMC pages were behind a browser check. I read PMC articles through the Europe PMC full-text API instead.

---

## Quick list: what to change or avoid

1. **Don't say "you lose heat 25 times faster in water."** The 25x figure is water's *thermal conductivity* compared with air. It is not how fast a body actually cools. An older physiology paper says the body cools about twice as fast in water as in air at the same temperature. I found no authoritative source for "4 times". Say "Water pulls heat from your body much faster than air at the same temperature." If you want a number, say "water conducts heat about 25 times better than air."
2. **1-10-1 is for ice-cold water.** Giesbrecht's numbers refer to near-freezing water. His "one hour to unconsciousness" also assumes a life jacket. Don't apply it to warm-ish water. Use it only as a "what if the water were cold" aside.
3. **Cold shock threshold:** the RNLI and Met Office say water **15 °C and below**. The response is strongest at 10–15 °C. The US National Weather Service says gasping can be triggered by water as warm as **25 °C (77 °F)**. The response peaks in the first ~30 s and settles over about 1–2 minutes (the RNLI says about 60–90 s).
4. **RNLI wording changed.** "Lean back, extend your arms and legs" is the older (2017–2020) wording. The current five steps start "Tilt your head back with ears submerged…". Use the current text, or paraphrase without calling it a quote.
5. **Drowning "is often", not "always", quiet.** Pia's Instinctive Drowning Response describes the final 20–60 s of struggle. Before that, a person in distress can still shout and wave. Newer video studies found more varied behaviour.
6. **Sweating:** an immersed body loses heat into the water, so don't make sweating the main way the character loses water. Say: no fresh water to replace losses, plus sun on the head and face, plus urine (being immersed itself makes the kidneys produce more urine).
7. **No exact survival times anywhere.** That includes "3 days without water", "X hours in water of Y °C", and USCG table values.
8. **Grimes case:** the Coast Guard said "at least 15 hours" (possibly more). "About 20 hours" is Grimes's and the media's estimate. The water was about 65–70 °F (18–21 °C), and he was mildly hypothermic. Attribute the times that way.
9. **USS Indianapolis survivors were not "with nothing".** Many had life jackets or rafts. The number killed by sharks is unknown (estimates run from a few dozen to about 150). Most men died of exposure, dehydration, saltwater drinking, wounds and exhaustion. Don't make it a "shark story".
10. **Kay Longstaff** fell from the Norwegian Star in **August 2018** (not 2019).
11. **Sharks:** avoid "shark-infested" and avoid saying sharks *will* come. Shark bites are rare: 65 confirmed unprovoked bites worldwide in 2025. Some rescued swimmers report a shark or "something" bumping them or coming close and leaving.
12. **Don't present drinking urine or small amounts of seawater as useful.** Alvarenga reported drinking urine. Experts quoted by National Geographic say it is not recommended.

## 1. Seawater averages about 3.5% salt
**Verdict: Accurate.**
NOAA: "The average salinity is about 35 parts per thousand… about 3.5 percent of the weight of seawater comes from the dissolved salts." Salinity varies a little by region.
**Suggested wording:** "On average, about 3.5% of seawater's weight is dissolved salt."
- https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/whysalty.html

## 2. Kidneys / drinking seawater worsens dehydration
**Verdict: Accurate** (and it is the NOAA page you remembered).
NOAA Ocean Service, "Can humans drink seawater?": "Human kidneys can only make urine that is less salty than salt water. Therefore, to get rid of all the excess salt taken in by drinking seawater, you have to urinate more water than you drank. Eventually, you die of dehydration even as you become thirstier."
**Suggested wording:** "Your kidneys can only make urine that's less salty than seawater. So to flush out the salt from every sip, your body has to pass more water than you drank. Seawater makes you *more* dehydrated."
- https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/drinksw.html
- Context: National Geographic (Duke physiologist Claude Piantadosi) says drinking urine is also not recommended, because it puts back salt the kidneys are trying to get rid of: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/140203-man-adrift-sea-survival-oceans-medicine-science

## 3. Cold shock response; temperatures; 1-10-1
**Verdict: Accurate in substance. Needs rewording on the temperature and timing details.**
- **What it is:** Tipton & Bradford (2014): the cold shock response "includes a gasp response, uncontrollable hyperventilation, tachycardia… it peaks in the first 30 s of immersion and adapts over the first 2 min. The loss of control of breathing on immersion can be a precursor to drowning." Eglin et al. (2015) found the reflex gasp happens "within the first 10 s of immersion". Tipton (1989) concluded cold shock "is probably responsible for the majority of annual open-water immersion deaths."
- **Temperatures:** Met Office (joint advice with the RNLI and RLSS UK): cold water shock is a response to sudden immersion "in water below 15°C". Tipton & Bradford: the most dangerous responses "peak between 10°C and 15°C". US National Weather Service: "Gasping for a breath or rapid breathing from sudden immersion can be triggered by water as warm as 77°F (25°C)". The NWS also says cold shock "can be just as severe and dangerous from water temperatures of 50-60°F (10-15°C)".
- **Duration:** the Met Office says "The initial effects typically last for around 90 seconds". An RNLI/Met Office page (RMetS) says "The initial effects of cold water pass in less than a minute".
- **1-10-1 (Gordon Giesbrecht, University of Manitoba):** 1 minute to get breathing under control; about 10 minutes of meaningful movement before cold incapacitation; about 1 hour before unconsciousness from hypothermia. In Giesbrecht's own Cold Water Boot Camp transcript, these figures are for near-freezing or ice water. He says "even in ice water, it'll take one hour before you become unconscious due to hypothermia—only if you have a life jacket on." He also says incapacitation "can occur within 2 to 10 minutes" in near-freezing water. Treat it as a memory aid, not a stopwatch.

**Suggested wording:** "If the water is cold, roughly 15 °C or below, falling in can trigger *cold shock*: an involuntary gasp and fast, uncontrolled breathing that is worst in the first 30 seconds or so and eases over a minute or two. Gasp with your face underwater and you can inhale water. That's why the first minute is so dangerous. Cold-water researcher Gordon Giesbrecht's rule for ice-cold water is '1-10-1': about one minute to get your breathing under control, about ten minutes of useful movement, and about an hour before hypothermia knocks you unconscious, and that hour assumes you're wearing a life jacket."
For the warm-ish scenario you could add: "In warm tropical water, cold shock is milder or absent. The danger here is slower."

## 4. RNLI "Float to Live", exact wording
**Verdict: Needs rewording.** Your version is the older wording. The current RNLI steps are different.

**Current RNLI wording (RNLI press releases 2024–2025, republished word for word):**
> - Tilt your head back with your ears submerged
> - Relax and try to breathe normally
> - Move your hands and legs to help you stay afloat
> - Remember that everyone floats differently and it is normal for legs to sink
> - Once your breathing is under control, call for help or swim to safety

A close variant (RMetS/Met Office, World Drowning Prevention Day): "Tilt your head back, submerging your ears. / Relax and try to control your breathing. / Move your hands to help you stay afloat. / Your legs may sink but that is OK – everyone floats differently. / Spread your arms and legs to improve your stability."

**Older RNLI wording (Respect the Water, about 2017–2020; still on the RNLI Jersey station site):**
> - Fight your instinct to thrash around
> - Lean back, extend your arms and legs
> - If you need to, gently move your arms and legs to help you float
> - Float until you can control your breathing
> - Only then call for help or swim to safety

Prof. Mike Tipton (University of Portsmouth, who worked with the RNLI on the advice): "the best immediate course of action is to fight your instinct and float on your back… most people can float… lean back, extend your arms and legs."

**Suggested narration (paraphrase, safe either way):** "Lifeboat crews have one piece of advice for anyone in trouble in the water: *Float to Live*. Tilt your head back with your ears underwater, relax, breathe, and spread your arms and legs. Your legs may sink, and that's OK. Once your breathing is under control, call for help or swim to safety."

- https://www.rmets.org/metmatters/world-drowning-prevention-day
- https://rnlijersey.org.je/safety-education/float-to-live (older wording)

## 5. Heat loss in water vs air: "25 times" or "4 times"?
**Verdict: Needs rewording.** "25 times" is defensible only as *thermal conductivity*. "4 times" has no authoritative source that I could find.
- Water's thermal conductivity is about 22–25 times that of air. The Transport Canada *Survival in Cold Waters* report (Brooks, 2003) says about 25x, and a Piantadosi book chapter says 22x. Water's volume-specific heat capacity is about 3,400 times that of air (Tipton & Bradford: "3,431 times that of air" at 37 °C).
- The *actual* whole-body cooling rate is a much smaller multiple, because vasoconstriction and the body's own tissue insulation limit heat flow. Robertson (1964, *Res Medica*, University of Edinburgh): "In water the body cools at just twice the speed that it would in air at the same temperature, although water conducts heat twenty-five times faster than air." The real rate also depends on water movement, swimming versus staying still, body fat and clothing.
- The NWS used to state "drains body heat up to 25 times faster than cold air". The current NWS cold-water page no longer gives a multiplier. The "4 times" figure appears only in local news (KOIN). I found it in no authoritative source.

**Safest wording:** "Water pulls heat out of your body much faster than air at the same temperature." Optional: "Water conducts heat about 25 times better than air."
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4052348/ (heat-capacity footnote)
- https://journals.ed.ac.uk/resmedica/article/view/419 (Robertson 1964; I read the PDF text directly)
- https://publications.gc.ca/site/eng/442356/publication.html (Transport Canada TP 13822)

## 6. Warm water still causes hypothermia eventually; cold seas much faster
**Verdict: Accurate, with rewording on the cold-water timing.**
- Tipton & Bradford: "'thermoneutral' water temperature (in which people can remain at rest and maintain deep body temperature without shivering or sweating) is within the narrow range of 35°C–35.5°C." Below that, a resting person slowly loses heat. Hypothermia means a core temperature below 35 °C (NWS).
- Real cases: John Aldridge was in about 75 °F (24 °C) Atlantic water for about 12 h and was "treated for dehydration, exposure and hypothermia". James Grimes was in about 65–70 °F (18–21 °C) Gulf water for 15+ h and was "mildly hypothermic, badly dehydrated" (NYT). The USCG's survival model for search planning (Probability of Survival Decision Aid) predicts survival time from both hypothermia *and* dehydration. A 2023 validation study immersed people in 18, 22 and 26 °C water for up to 10 h.
- Cold water: Giesbrecht's "about an hour to unconsciousness" is for ice water with a life jacket. Cold shock and loss of muscle control kill much sooner.

**Suggested wording:** "Even tropical-feeling water is colder than your body. You only stay in balance at rest in water around 35 °C. Hour after hour, the ocean slowly drains your heat, and rescued swimmers have been treated for hypothermia after a night in water most people would call warm. In ice-cold water, hypothermia can make you lose consciousness in about an hour, and cold shock and numb muscles strike long before that."
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4052348/
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37865983/ (Castellani et al. 2023, USCG PSDA)
- https://www.danspapers.com/2013/08/one-week-later-the-full-story-of-the-heroic-rescue-of-montauk-fisherman-john-aldridge

## 7. Drowning is usually quiet (Instinctive Drowning Response)
**Verdict: Accurate if hedged.** Use "often/usually", not "always".
Frank (Francesco) Pia, writing in the Coast Guard's *On Scene* magazine (quoted by retired USCG rescue swimmer Mario Vittone): "Except in rare circumstances, drowning people are physiologically unable to call out for help… Drowning people cannot wave for help. Nature instinctively forces them to extend their arms laterally and press down on the water's surface… these people can only struggle on the surface of the water from 20 to 60 seconds before submersion occurs."
Tipton & Montgomery (2022) call the IDR "the most widely accepted description of visible drowning behaviour" (no calling or waving, airway repeatedly submerged, upright body). They add that a 2020s video study found "a large variety of complex visible behaviours". Before that final phase, a person in *distress* may still shout and wave.

**Suggested wording:** "Real drowning rarely looks like the movies. In the final struggle, a person usually can't shout or wave. Their mouth keeps slipping under, and their arms press down on the water just to grab a breath. It can be over in under a minute, often in near silence."
- https://soundingsonline.com/?p=12285 (Vittone, "Drowning doesn't look like drowning", quoting Pia/On Scene)
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34791956/ (Tipton & Montgomery, "The experience of drowning", *Medico-Legal Journal* 2022; full text PMC8928428)

## 8. Exhaustion is a major factor without flotation
**Verdict: Accurate.**
Tipton & Montgomery (2022): for people who can swim, the drowning struggle "will not start until swim failure begins. This may take hours in warm water and, in this scenario, be associated with exhaustion." In cold water, swim failure can come in as little as about 10 minutes. On Grimes, the USCG rescue swimmer said: "Mr. Grimes had nothing left. He had no energy… he had between a minute and 30 seconds left before we lost him completely." He also said Grimes "didn't have flotation (devices), he didn't have radio or flares." In the US in 2024, where life-jacket use was known, 87% of boating victims were not wearing one (USCG).

**Suggested wording:** "Without anything to float on, the real enemy is exhaustion. Every kick and stroke burns energy you can't replace. In warm water a strong swimmer can keep going for hours, but sooner or later the body gives out. That's why floating calmly, instead of fighting the water, matters so much."
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34791956/
- https://www.fox29.com/news/cruise-ship-passenger-overboard-gulf-of-mexico-treaded-water-20-hours-speaks

## 9. Survival without water: days, not weeks
**Verdict: Accurate.** "Days, not weeks" is defensible. Don't give a number.
*Scientific American* (Prof. Randall Packer, George Washington University): "It is impossible to give an answer to this seemingly simple question." In extreme heat with exertion, people can "dehydrate, overheat and die in a period of a few hours". A resting adult in comfortable conditions "can survive for a week or more with no, or very limited, water intake." National Geographic (Piantadosi): "Water is the critical limiting factor for survival." People can generally last "five to six weeks without food."

**Suggested wording:** "Without fresh water, survival is counted in days, not weeks, and heat, sun and effort shorten that. Food can wait; water can't."
- https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-long-can-the-average/
- https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/140203-man-adrift-sea-survival-oceans-medicine-science

## 10. Dehydration/exhaustion cause confusion, delirium, hallucinations; castaway reports
**Verdict: Accurate, with careful attribution for the Indianapolis case.**
- MedlinePlus (NIH) lists severe dehydration symptoms including "Irritability or confusion", "Listlessness", "Unconsciousness or delirium". Delirium involves "changes in… perception". Piantadosi (NatGeo): dehydration and sun exposure can cause confusion.
- USS Indianapolis (sunk 30 July 1945): of 1,195 aboard, about 900 reached the water and 316 survived after about 4–5 days. Smithsonian: some men "suffered hallucinations that compelled them to drink the seawater around them". National Geographic: some drank seawater, "which caused salt poisoning and mass hallucinations". One survivor described men all seeing the same imaginary island. Note that these men had life jackets and some rafts, and the causes were combined: dehydration, seawater, exhaustion, exposure, injuries.

**Suggested wording:** "As thirst and exhaustion build, the mind starts to fray: confusion, then delirium, sometimes hallucinations. Survivors of the USS Indianapolis, adrift for days in 1945, described men seeing islands and ships that weren't there."
- https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000982.htm
- https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/sinking-uss-indianapolis-triggered-worst-shark-attack-history-25715092/
- https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/uss-indianapolis-shark-attack-jaws

## 11. Water reflects sunlight/UV; heat increases sweating
**Verdict: First half accurate (keep it modest). Second half needs rewording.**
- EPA: "Water, snow and sand reflect the damaging rays of the sun, which can increase your chance of sunburn." WHO's Global Solar UV Index guide (2002) gives reflection figures of about 80% for fresh snow, about 15% for dry sand and about 25% for sea foam. Calm water reflects less (around 10% or less, per secondary sources). I saw these WHO numbers only in search excerpts, so don't quote them in narration.
- Sweating: an immersed body is *losing* heat to the water, so heavy sweating isn't the main water loss. Only the sun-baked head and face can overheat. Better to name all the losses: breath, some sweat, and urine. Head-out immersion itself raises urine output (Nakamitsu et al. 1994: urine flow rose about sixfold during 5-h immersion in near-thermoneutral water). A 2023 USCG-related study found the survival model *under-predicted* water loss during warm-water immersion by 84%.

**Suggested wording:** "Above the waterline, the sun beats down on your face, and the bright surface and sea foam bounce some of that UV back up, so sunburn comes fast. Meanwhile your body keeps losing water, through your breath, your skin, and even your kidneys, which make more urine when you're immersed. And there's nothing to drink."
- https://www.epa.gov/sunsafety/action-steps-sun-safety
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7836219/ (immersion diuresis)
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37865983/ (water loss in warm-water immersion)
- WHO Global Solar UV Index guide (archived): https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20130216222323/http://www.who.int/uv/publications/en/GlobalUVI.pdf

## 12. Sharks
**Verdict: Accurate if worded carefully.**
- Florida Museum (oceanic whitetip profile): these sharks "are known to have attacked survivors of ship and plane wrecks at sea" and "show little fear and are often quite persistent circling around divers". NOAA Fisheries: oceanic whitetips live "offshore in the open ocean" near the surface. The species is now **threatened** after steep declines.
- USS Indianapolis: experts generally attribute the shark attacks to oceanic whitetips and possibly tiger sharks. The number killed by sharks is unknown, with estimates from a few dozen to about 150. The sharks mostly fed on the dead and dying at first. National Geographic stresses it was "much more than just a shark story".
- Rarity: the International Shark Attack File counted **65 confirmed unprovoked bites worldwide in 2025** and says the number "remains extremely low". (These figures are mostly from beaches, but they show how rare bites are overall.)
- "Investigate and leave": Aldridge reported fins nearby, and a review of his book says sharks came within about 10 feet and swam away. Grimes said something bumped his leg but did not bite. These are anecdotes, not statistics.
- Splashing: Florida Museum quick tips include "Avoid excess splashing" and "Don't swim around schools of fish". The second is relevant to item 13.

**Suggested wording:** "Sharks live out here too. Open-ocean species like the oceanic whitetip have attacked shipwreck survivors in the past, most infamously after the USS Indianapolis sank in 1945. But shark bites on people are rare, and a shark that comes close is often just curious. Some rescued swimmers say sharks circled or bumped them and moved on. Staying calm and avoiding wild splashing is the best you can do."
- https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/discover-fish/species-profiles/carcharhinus-longimanus/
- https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/shark-attacks/yearly-worldwide-summary/
- https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/shark-attacks/reduce-risk/quick-tips/
- https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/oceanic-whitetip-shark

## 13. Fish gather under floating objects (FAD principle)
**Verdict: Accurate.**
University of Hawaii (HIMB) FAD program: "pelagic fishes will aggregate to floating objects like logs, nets, and other debris". Researchers still don't fully know why. NOAA: FADs "are floating objects that are designed and strategically placed to attract pelagic fish." A review (Castro et al. 2002, *Rev Fish Biol Fisheries*, doi:10.1023/A:1020302414472) proposes that floating objects shelter eggs, larvae and juvenile fish. Castaway Steven Callahan was shadowed by schooling dorado under his raft, and in 1982 fishermen found him because frigate birds were feeding on that school (*Christian Science Monitor*, 1982).

**Suggested wording:** "In the open ocean, anything that floats becomes a landmark. Little fish gather under drifting logs, nets, even life rafts, and bigger fish follow. Fishers use this trick on purpose with buoys called fish aggregating devices." For the lone swimmer, say "fish may gather", not "will".
- https://www.himb.hawaii.edu/FADS/FADFAQ.html
- https://marinedebris.noaa.gov/what-are-fish-aggregating-devices-fads
- https://www.csmonitor.com/1982/0930/093036.html

## 14. Bioluminescent plankton glow when disturbed, including open ocean
**Verdict: Accurate.**
Letendre et al. (2024, *Frontiers in Marine Science*, review co-authored by Michael Latz, Scripps): mechanically stimulated bioluminescence "can often be seen on beaches with breaking waves, or in the wake of a moving ship or swimming nekton". The flash is triggered when waves, eddies or currents stress the cell membrane. The review gives open-ocean examples: *Pyrocystis noctiluca* caused "up to 30% of the bioluminescence measured at stations in the Sargasso Sea", and *Noctiluca scintillans* is "a major contributor to bioluminescence from tropical oceans to northern seas." NOAA Ocean Exploration: bioluminescence "is very common in the ocean, at least in the pelagic zone." The glow is usually patchier and dimmer offshore than in famous coastal "glowing bays". Not all dinoflagellates glow, and other plankton (such as gelatinous animals) also glow.

**Suggested wording:** "At night, every kick may leave a trail of blue-green sparks. Tiny plankton, including dinoflagellates, flash when the water around them is disturbed."
- https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2023.1299602/full
- https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/facts/bioluminescence.html

## 15. Real cases

### James Michael Grimes, Carnival Valor, Gulf of Mexico (23–24 Nov 2022). In the water, no flotation.
- Last seen about 11 p.m. Wednesday 23 Nov at the ship's bar. Reported missing the next day. The ship called the Coast Guard about 2:30 p.m. Thursday.
- Spotted by the crew of the bulk carrier **Crinis**. Hoisted by a Coast Guard MH-60 Jayhawk at **8:25 p.m.** Thursday (Thanksgiving), about **20 miles south of Southwest Pass, Louisiana**. Taken to New Orleans Lakefront Airport, stable condition (USCG release).
- **Time in water:** the Coast Guard said "at least 15 hours" (NY Daily News citing the USCG), and Lt. Seth Gross said "could have been more than 15". Grimes and later media said "nearly 20 hours". Narration: "at least 15 hours, by the Coast Guard's estimate".
- **Water:** about 65–70 °F (18–21 °C) per Coast Guard officials (NYT). Rain, wind, 3–5 ft waves.
- **Condition:** "mildly hypothermic, badly dehydrated" (NYT). CBS: "responsive but exhibiting hypothermic-like symptoms". Rescue swimmer Richard Hoefle: "Mr. Grimes had nothing left… between a minute and 30 seconds left," and he "didn't have flotation (devices), he didn't have radio or flares."
- **What helped:** he kept swimming and treading water. He said he was strong-willed ("dead set on making it out of there") and kept faith and hope that people would search. The Coast Guard broadcast an alert to all mariners, and an alert merchant crew spotted him. His family described him as a strong swimmer. He fought jellyfish and currents, and something bumped his leg but did not bite (ABC).

### John Aldridge, lobsterman off Montauk, NY (24 July 2013). In the water, no life jacket.
- Fell from the lobster boat *Anna Mary* at night, about 40 miles offshore. His crewmates were asleep, and the Coast Guard was alerted hours later.
- Stayed afloat about **12 hours** (USCG spokesperson, gCaptain). He upended his rubber boots and used them as floats under his arms, then cut loose lobster-pot buoys to hold on to.
- The search covered about 660–780 square miles with Coast Guard boats, aircraft and volunteer fishing boats. According to the NYT Magazine story and his book, searchers passed without seeing him.
- Found by a Coast Guard helicopter about 36–43 miles south of Montauk. Treated for "dehydration, exposure and hypothermia", in water of about **75 °F (24 °C)**.

### Kay Longstaff, Norwegian Star, Adriatic Sea (August 2018). In the water.
- British, 46. Fell overboard about midnight. Rescued about **10 hours** later (09:40) by the Croatian coast guard vessel *Cavtat*, about a mile from where she fell. Hypothermic but in "good physical shape" (Croatian Ministry of Maritime Affairs). She said yoga had kept her fit and that she sang to stay warm and awake.

### Castaways (in a raft or boat, so contrast them with the "nothing" scenario)
- **Steven Callahan (1982):** his 21-ft sloop *Napoleon Solo* was swamped (probably by a whale) around 4 February 1982 after he left the Canary Islands. He drifted **76 days** in an inflatable raft. He made drinking water with solar stills and caught rain, and ate mostly dorado that schooled under the raft. Fishermen rescued him near Marie-Galante (Guadeloupe, French West Indies) on 21 April 1982. Source: *Christian Science Monitor* 1982 https://www.csmonitor.com/1982/0930/093036.html
- **Poon Lim (1942–43):** steward on the British merchant ship SS *Benlomond*, torpedoed 23 Nov 1942. He survived alone on a wooden raft for **133 days** (some 1943 reports said 131–132). He caught fish and seabirds and collected rainwater, and was rescued by Brazilian fishermen on 5 April 1943. He received the British Empire Medal. Sources: London Gazette https://thegazette.co.uk/London/issue/36091/page/3169/data.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poon_Lim
- **José Salvador Alvarenga (2012–14):** left the Mexican coast on 17 Nov 2012 on a fishing trip in a roughly 24-ft open boat. He drifted across the Pacific and reached Ebon Atoll, Marshall Islands, on **30 Jan 2014** (often quoted as 438 days). His companion Ezequiel Córdoba died months into the drift. Alvarenga reported eating raw fish, birds and turtles and drinking rainwater, turtle blood and urine. Experts called his story "plausible" (Piantadosi), but some were skeptical at first. Sources: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/140203-man-adrift-sea-survival-oceans-medicine-science ; https://www.nbcnews.com/storyline/marshall-islands-castaway/exclusive-interview-real-life-castaway-details-epic-survival-n21081

## 16. Key factors for realistic survival chances
**Verdict: Accurate.** All are supported.
- **Water temperature:** it decides whether cold shock, incapacitation and hypothermia come in minutes or over many hours (Tipton & Bradford; NWS; Giesbrecht).
- **Flotation:** USCG 2024 statistics: "Where cause of death was known, 76% of fatal boating incident victims drowned. Where Personal Flotation Device usage was known, 87% were not wearing a Personal Floatation Device." Giesbrecht's "one hour" in ice water also assumes a life jacket.
- **Someone knowing you're missing:** Grimes wasn't reported until the next day. Aldridge's crew discovered he was gone only hours later, and the first search area was wrong. Rescue started only when someone raised the alarm.
- **Visibility:** a head in the waves is tiny. Searchers passed Aldridge without seeing him, and Grimes was spotted by an alert merchant-ship crew.
- **Signalling devices:** NOAA SARSAT says a 406 MHz PLB/EPIRB alerts rescuers "within minutes of activation". GPS-encoded models give location to about 100 m. Carrying one "is sometimes the only hope for survival." The Coast Guard rescuer noted Grimes had "no radio or flares".
- **Also relevant:** the USCG plans search duration with a survival model (PSDA) that accounts for hypothermia and dehydration (Castellani et al. 2023).
- Sources: https://uscgboating.org/library/accident-statistics/Recreational-Boating-Statistics-2024.pdf ; https://www.sarsat.noaa.gov/emergency-406-beacons/ ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37865983/

**Suggested closing wording:** "What decides whether someone like this makes it home? How cold the water is. Whether they have something that floats. Whether anyone knows they're missing and is looking. Whether searchers can spot them. And whether they can call for help, with a beacon, a radio or a flare. Out here, a life jacket and a locator beacon can turn days of luck into hours of waiting."
