# Science fact sheet

> Updated after an adversarial multi-agent fact-check. See `factcheck_report.md`; the wording below follows the original script, and the corrected lines are in `narration.txt`.

 — "Could you survive trapped in a giant industrial freezer?"

Every claim in the narration, what it is based on, and how it was kept careful.

| Narration claim | Basis | Care taken |
|---|---|---|
| Walk-in / industrial freezers run around **−20 °C** | Commercial walk-in freezers are typically held at about −18 to −23 °C (0 to −10 °F); blast freezers go colder (−30 to −40 °C). | Uses a typical storage value, not a blast freezer. |
| Fans keep moving the frozen air | Evaporator units in walk-ins circulate air with fans; moving air increases convective heat loss (the basis of wind chill). | — |
| You feel cold within seconds, but that's skin nerves; your core is still ~37 °C | Feeling cold comes from skin thermoreceptors signalling the hypothalamus; hypothermia is defined by **core** temperature below 35 °C. | Separates "feeling cold" from hypothermia, as the brief asked. |
| The body squeezes skin blood vessels, pulling warm blood away from the hands | Peripheral vasoconstriction is the first defence against cold; it protects the core at the cost of the extremities (pale, numb, clumsy fingers). | — |
| Shivering: rapid muscle twitches that burn energy to make heat | Shivering can raise heat production several-fold over resting level. | No wattage or % figures given. |
| Heat escapes into the air, with every breath, and into anything you touch | Convection/radiation to cold air, respiratory heat and moisture loss, conduction into cold surfaces. | No disputed percentage split quoted. |
| Fingers go numb and clumsy | Cold tissue and slowed nerve conduction reduce dexterity. | — |
| Thick metal walls block your signal | Insulated metal-skinned panels often block mobile reception. | Shown as one plausible situation, not a universal rule. |
| Most walk-in freezers have an emergency release inside the door | Manufacturers fit interior safety releases (push knob / mushroom cap / panic bar) so the door can be opened from inside. | Says "most", not "all"; local codes vary. |
| …but this one is jammed with ice | WorkSafe NB hazard alert: ice build-up around a mushroom-cap handle's push rod trapped a worker inside a freezer. | A real, documented failure mode. |
| Below 35 °C core = hypothermia | Standard clinical definition (core < 35 °C). | — |
| Mild stage: violent shivering, fumbling, slurred speech, poor judgement | Mild hypothermia (35–32 °C): shivering, ataxia, dysarthria, impaired judgement. | — |
| Best move: get off the metal floor (conduction), sit on cardboard, hands in armpits, curl up | Insulating yourself from cold surfaces and reducing exposed surface area are standard cold-survival advice. | Practical, non-medical advice only. |
| Nearing 32 °C the shivering can stop — not because you're warm | Shivering typically fades around 30–32 °C as the body's heat-producing defence fails (moderate hypothermia 32–28 °C). | Says "can", not "will". |
| Confusion and drowsiness; you may not realise you're in danger | Impaired consciousness, apathy and confusion are hallmarks of moderate hypothermia. | — |
| Below about 28 °C people usually lose consciousness; heart rhythm can become dangerously unstable | Severe hypothermia (< 28 °C): unconsciousness, bradycardia, risk of ventricular fibrillation. | "About" and "usually"; cut-offs differ slightly between staging systems. |
| In cold air serious hypothermia usually takes hours, not minutes — but there's no fixed countdown | Air conducts heat far more slowly than water (water ~25× faster), so cooling in air is much slower than in cold water. | **No survival time is stated.** The video never shows a clock or elapsed time. |
| It depends on temperature, moving air, clothing, staying dry, body and health, how fast you're found | Cold-stress guidance (CDC/NIOSH-style toolbox material): wind, wetness, clothing, age, health, alcohol and exhaustion all change risk. | Presented as factors, not a formula. |
| Doctors have revived people from astonishingly low temperatures; "nobody's dead until they're warm and dead" | Documented recoveries from severe accidental hypothermia with extracorporeal rewarming (e.g. the 1999 Bågenholm case); the maxim is widely used in emergency medicine. | Framed as a saying, not a promise. |

## On-screen numbers
The CORE thermometer shows illustrative values that move through the stage thresholds the narration names
(37 → 35 → 32 → 28 °C) with labels NORMAL / HYPOTHERMIA / MILD / MODERATE / SEVERE. No elapsed time
appears anywhere, so no specific survival timeline is implied. The time-lapse is labelled "HOURS PASS…",
which matches the narration ("Hours can pass").

## Sources consulted
- StatPearls, *Hypothermia* (NCBI Bookshelf): staging, shivering cessation at ~30–32 °C, signs by stage.
- Scandinavian Journal of Trauma, Resuscitation and Emergency Medicine (2016), accidental hypothermia staging table.
- WorkSafe New Brunswick hazard alert, "Frozen 'mushroom cap' handle traps worker inside freezer".
- KaTom / KPS Global walk-in safety guidance (interior release mechanisms, alarms).
- Operandio / ChefsDeal walk-in freezer temperature guides (−18 to −23 °C).
- CDC MMWR cold-stress editorial notes; USDA-ARS / Princeton EHS cold-stress fact sheets (risk factors).
- Gilbert et al. / Norwegian ECLS hypothermia series ("Nobody is dead until warm and dead", PubMed 24882104).
- Rothschild & Schneider 1995, Swedish registry (paradoxical undressing; consulted, not used in the script).
